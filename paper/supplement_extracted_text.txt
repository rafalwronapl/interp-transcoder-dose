1 Introduction
A method can predict small perturbations poorly yet outperform local linearization on large fi-
nite interventions. For transcoders used in mechanistic interpretability, that possibility creates a
practical question:when does poor local response fidelity coexist with useful prediction of a native
downstream effect, and what properties of the prediction error support that regime?
We study a public Gemma-3-270M transcoder at layer 15, with fixed donor edits propagated
through the remaining native network. A historically selected pronoun-readout result motivated
a new test on 256 disjoint name-pair blocks. The held-out result is a dose-dependent ranking
crossover: native Taylor is better at dose .05, whereas the transcoder is better at a full donor patch,
including comparison with a FIT-trained ridge map and on a predeclared transfer template. This is
accuracy of a predicted readout effect, not improved natural answers or validation of a replacement
circuit. Here native Taylor uses the native branch Jacobian to predict a vector change, which is
then propagated through the exact nonlinear native suffix; it is neither the transcoder’s Jacobian
nor a first-order approximation of the final scalar readout.
A second public artifact, Gemma-3-1B layer 22, separates two claims that the first result alone
could conflate. On 256 additional disjoint blocks, Taylor again wins at small dose and loses to the
transcoder at larger doses. Yet at the full donor patch the transcoder is worse than zero change,
ridge and the FIT class mean. Useful high accuracy therefore does not follow from beating Taylor.
The full curve also matters: at dose 2 the transcoder beats zero change and the class mean, but
still loses to ridge.
We investigate the first artifact’s residual geometry using existing states rather than another
fresh confirmation. A new 32-block experiment substitutes whole residuals from the same dose and
condition with matched norms; a separate mask intervention tests FIT-selected switching groups.
These controls avoid the dose-independent baseline mismatch of our earlier 16-block substitution
study. They constrain the effect of specified residual directions and feature-mask changes, while un-
equal actual switching strength prevents interpreting the selected-versus-random contrast as isolated
feature identity or a native circuit. The complete-cache diagnosis and earlier experiment remain
separately reported secondary evidence. We now distinguish failed unscaled disjoint-group match-
ing from a changed, equal-vector-norm estimand. A fresh lexical cohort tests the latter with fixed
groups and scale; it descriptively reproduces a prediction-direction contrast on the same artifact,
without a prespecified inferential decision or a complete explanation of crossover.
A separate Qwen3-0.6B component test provides a boundary: its measured CLT delta remains
near the zero-change baseline across the dose curve. Gemma and Qwen differ in artifact, layer,
suffix and readout, so their contrast cannot isolate a model-family cause. Historical failed forecasts
and selected controls are retained in the appendices; they are not pooled with the fresh test or the
posthoc mechanism sample as independent replications.

2 Related work
Transcoder-based circuits and their validation are established topics [4, 1]. JumpReLU has an
explicitly discontinuous threshold [10]; threshold crossings are therefore not a newly discovered
property. Computational sparsity is also the object of Jacobian sparse autoencoders [5], although
sparsifying a Jacobian is different from matching a native MLP’s Jacobian. Shabalin and Piotrowski
directly examine transcoder-feature correspondence and Jacobian fidelity [11].
JSAEs also compare Jacobians with finite latent interventions and discuss threshold-crossing
exceptions; their SAE-decoder–MLP–SAE-encoder map differs from our native-versus-transcoder
delta comparison [5]. Ladner and Althoff study MLP–replacement discrepancy over continuous sets
of internal activations using adversarial lower bounds and formal upper bounds [7]. Our baseline-
subtracted dose response and native-suffix readouts address a different estimand. These are already
established neighboring questions, not newly discovered objections to this study.
Zhang et al. show that intervention-strength controls can change comparisons across sparse
autoencoder codes [12]. Their code-refit and output-calibration experiments differ from our fixed
public-transcoder predictions of native MLP changes. The same coefficientcneed not give equal
full- and projected-edit norms; our operator comparisons therefore describe the specified edits rather
than an isolated causal effect of operator identity.
Zhang and Wang analyze failures of first-order attribution patching and propose a second-order
correction for downstream nonlinearity [13]. Our native Taylor comparator instead linearizes the
branch’s vector response and then applies the exact nonlinear native suffix. Its finite-dose error
cannot therefore be attributed to replacing that suffix by a scalar first-order approximation; the
approximated branch vector can still lead to a large readout error. The two baselines address
related but distinct approximations.
Bazán Sánchez tests whether a measured midpoint feature ablation predicts complete removal
in transcoder replacement systems [2]. That already provides a precedent for finite-dose prediction
and frozen held-out forecasts. Its endpoint is a target feature’s preactivation inside the replacement
model; here the reference is a native branch change scored through the remaining native network.
Our public TC prediction does not use a measured intermediate TEST intervention effect as its
input. Neither comparison by itself establishes a complete causal mechanism.
Our central empirical contribution is a held-out dose-dependent reversal between public
transcoders and native-vector Taylor, with an important separation between rank and accuracy.
The selected 270M artifact also beats FIT-trained ridge at the full patch and transfers across
3
templates; the 1B artifact repeats the crossover but does not repeat that accuracy advantage.
Same-dose residual substitutions and restricted mask changes provide conditional diagnostics of
the first artifact, not a training-cause identification. The negative Qwen component delimits the
evidence further. We do not claim invention of Jacobian comparison, replacement-model validation,
or attribution-versus-intervention testing. Novelty is bounded by the reviewed literature, not

Lange et al. show that cross-layer transcoder objectives can favor shortcuts that omit inter-

negative Qwen follow-up measures one local source-27–target-27 component. It neither evaluates
the whole CLT graph nor establishes such shortcuts as the cause of that component’s errors.

4 Held-out Gemma test: a dose-dependent crossover

medium public transcoder, the six-dose grid and both input-edit operators before TEST. Its vector-
only FIT used 32 name-pair blocks and 128 natural states across so/when; it fixed the class-mean

documents in total. The primary descriptive summaries below use the two original forms, 1024
documents. The selected artifact and task regime remain conditional choices, not a random sample
of models or readouts.

6.2 Restricting feature-mask changes
Leta 0, a1 be preactivations,m 0, m1 the corresponding JumpReLU threshold masks, andu 1 =
ReLU(a1). Defineh base =u 1m0 ands=u 1(m1 −m 0). For a groupG, ONLY useshbase + 1Gsand
EXCEPT usesh base + (1−1 G)s. Each code is decoded with the original decoder/bias, baseline-
subtracted and scored through the native suffix. Endpoint magnitudes and nonswitching features
remain; ONLY doesnotmean a 32-feature circuit. BASE can differ from the archived frozen-mask
linear predictor because of endpoint ReLU clamping.
The group contains the top 32 FIT-scored features by mean absolute switch contribution times
decoder-row norm; a fixed-seed random group of 32 is drawn from its complement. No TEST
outcomes select either group. At full dose/full edits, ONLY-top yields .10463 MSE versus the
original .11153, whereas EXCEPT-top yields 8.93294. Thus the selected group’s mask changes
approximately preserve prediction accuracy under this intervention, and removing them is highly
damaging. Projected full-dose values are .11782 and 7.45841 versus .12119 originally. At small dose
removing the group instead slightly improves pooled loss; we do not promote only the favorable
conditions.
The random comparison isnot strength matched. At full dose/full edits, the selected group
switches an average 8.3125 features versus .02083 for random; random has zero switches in 188/192
contexts. Projected full-dose random is inactive in 191/192 contexts. Equal group cardinality
therefore does not isolate feature identity at equal actual switch count or intervention norm. These
bounded posthoc diagnostics establish conditional importance of specified mask changes in the
replacement prediction, not faithful native features, a native circuit, or an additive percentage
explanation of the crossover.

9 Discussion and conclusion
The strongest positive result remains the 270M held-out full-patch prediction and predeclared
template transfer. The second artifact changes the generalization claim: the Taylor–TC rank-
ing crossover repeats, but useful full-patch accuracy does not. Atc= 1its TC loses to zero change,
ridge and mean despite beating Taylor; atc= 2it beats zero and mean but not ridge. Reporting the
11
full curve avoids conflating these outcomes. All methods within a condition have the same relative-
error denominator; the ranking reversal also occurs in absolute error. Neither artifact establishes
decreasing absolute TC error with dose or general replacement-model faithfulness.
The bounded posthoc oracle diagnostics using same-dose, norm-matched whole-residual substi-
tutions show favorable original orientation in every full-dose frame against two partners, with a
small-dose exception retained. The selected-switch test shows that a FIT-selected group’s mask
changes preserve much of the first artifact’s full-patch accuracy under the specified replacement
operation. Its nearly inactive random control cannot isolate identity at equal strength, and the
experiment preserves endpoint magnitudes and other features. It therefore provides a bounded in-
tervention diagnosis, not a recovered native circuit. The new norm-rescaled direction test addresses
one strength confound by changing the intervention, and fresh lexical data descriptively reproduce
its positive prediction-damage contrast. No formal significance, confidence-interval or prespecified
decision claim is attached to this follow-up. Failed unscaled matching remains a distinct negative
result. The restricted transfer and especially object eligibility constrain generalization; equal out-
put norm still does not equalize switch count, semantic content or curvature. The earlier 16-block
baseline-error substitution is a different construction with a dose-independent mismatch and sub-
stantial interactions; its operator-dependent findings remain secondary rather than being combined
into an additive mechanism account.
Scope and inference remain explicit. Both public Gemma artifacts had historical exposure;
their new lexical tests use fixed acquisition protocols. The 32- and 16-block diagnostics reuse
observed data. The Qwen component remains a negative boundary, not an architecture-controlled
comparison. Synthetic contexts, selected branches and two-token readouts do not measure general
answer quality. The original hypothesis family failed calibration; separately qualified 270M intervals
cover only their own metrics, while the second-artifact intervals are nominal pointwise estimates
conditional on FIT. Shared templates and repeated measurements do not enlarge the number of
independent blocks. Historical failures and all new unfavorable conditions are retained.
Local fidelity, ranking against a local approximation, useful finite-effect accuracy and a mech-
anistic explanation are separate empirical claims. The evidence supports the first artifact’s con-
ditional usefulness and a repeated ranking crossover, while limiting both generalization and the
interpretation of its switching and residual geometry.

I Prospective chronology and inferential scope
The artifact/regime was selected historically. New lexical splits, directions, baselines, operators,
doses and readouts were frozen before the new TEST. The final acquisition was explicitly autho-
rized before TEST as a fixedN= 256descriptive study, retaining the originally defined scientific
contrasts while declining population hypothesis-support decisions from an unqualified test family.
This chronology does not retroactively make historical selected results confirmatory.
The original statistical qualification required all synthetic scenarios to have upper endpoints of
simultaneous Clopper–Pearson intervals for family-wise false rejection at most 0.06. The implemen-
tation constructs two-sided intervals and uses their upper endpoint. AtN= 256, 52/54 scenarios
passed; the two failures had empirical rates 4.20% and 4.25%, with upper bounds 6.0728% and
6.1317%. Thus qualification was not obtained, but those counts do not establish that the true error
rate exceeds 5%. BothN= 128andN= 256remained ineligible under that rule. We retain
that outcome rather than relaxing the cutoff after observing it. The manuscript consequently uses
measured finite-corpus errors and contrasts without new family p-values, Holm rejection labels or
population confirmation claims.
A separate interval-method amendment, chosen before B TEST access, uses paired studen-
tized log-RMS max-statistic intervals. All eight fixed synthetic cases passed its finite-scenario
joint-coverage qualification. This is not a distribution-free guarantee, qualification of the origi-
nal hypothesis-test family, or evidence that the desired interval widths were achieved on TEST. The
actual intervals are now reported; one of the twelve width targets fails, so joint precision attainment
is not claimed. Scope is limited to the predefined 12 TC metrics atc= 1; it does not automatically
extend to every dose or comparator.
