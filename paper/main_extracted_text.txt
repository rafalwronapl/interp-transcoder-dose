A Dose-Dependent Crossover in Transcoder Fidelity:
Beating Local Linearization Is Not Finite-Effect Accuracy
Rafał Wrona
Independent researcher
November 2026
Abstract
A public transcoder can be a poor local model of a native MLP and still predict the down-
stream effect of a large intervention accurately. On 256 fresh name-pair blocks, a fixed Gemma-
3-270M layer-15 transcoder predicts the pronoun-readout effect of a full donor patch with 10.11%
relative RMS error, versus 129.96% for native-vector Taylor and 28.05% for a ridge map fitted
to native vector changes, although that ridge map has the smaller vector error. At dose .05
the ranking reverses: the transcoder is worse than predicting no change (117.46%), Taylor has
12.10%. The large-dose advantage persists for projected edits that do not reach the donor state,
at twice the donor difference, and on a held-out template. A second public artifact, Gemma-3-1B
layer 22, repeats the Taylor–transcoder crossover but not the accuracy: at the full patch it loses
to zero change and to ridge. Outperforming local linearization is therefore not sufficient evi-
dence of finite-effect accuracy; the relevant test is dose-resolved readout accuracy against learned
and zero-change baselines. The favorable artifact was selected after an exploratory sweep and
then tested on fresh data. We further characterize its errors through endpoint cancellation and
the threshold switches of a FIT-selected feature group, and report that a single source-layer
component of a Qwen cross-layer transcoder stays at the zero-change baseline.
1 Introduction
Transcoders and cross-layer transcoders are increasingly used as replacement models: to read cir-
cuits, to choose features to steer, and to predict what an edit will do [4, 1]. Their fidelity is usually
judged through reconstruction, first-order Jacobian agreement [10] or interventions at one chosen
strength [1], while the use cases involve finite interventions of varying size. These two regimes need
not agree. First-order approximations can misestimate full patches [11] and break down at large
steering scales [6], but in the literature we reviewed we found no measurement of how a sparse
replacement’s predicted intervention effects behave across that range, relative to the native model’s
own linearization.
We measure it. For a fixed public transcoder we sweep the intervention dose from a small fraction
of a donor difference to twice the full patch, propagate every prediction through the exact native
downstream network, and score the predicted change of a two-token readout. On Gemma-3-270M
layer 15 we find a clean crossover: native Taylor is far better at small dose, the transcoder far better
at the full patch, where it also beats a ridge map fitted to native vector changes, despite that map’s
smaller vector error: the advantage is specific to the downstream readout. The artifact was chosen
after an exploratory sweep in which it stood out; the result holds on a fresh lexical TEST frozen in
advance, on a held-out template, for projected edits and at dose 2.

two artifacts differ in it.
Diagnostics on the first artifact then ask what its favorable error looks like. Endpoint recon-

2 Related work
Transcoder circuits and their validation are established [4, 1]. Shabalin and Piotrowski [10] compare
transcoder and native Jacobians, stating that their faithfulness analysis is first-order rather than
under interventions (their case studies include ablation and steering); formal discrepancy bounds [7]
cover activation sets rather than intervention effects. Circuit-tracing validations perturb features
at chosen strengths [1] but do not resolve accuracy by dose against native linearization; that gap
is the subject of this paper. Jacobian sparse autoencoders [5] sparsify computation rather than
test a replacement’s finite response, and JumpReLU thresholds [9] make discontinuous responses
expected.
On the linearization side, attribution patching can misestimate the effect of full activation
patches [11], and downstream nonlinearity motivates second-order corrections [13]. Guo et al. [6]
show that first-order intervention attribution agrees in sign with realized steering responses at a
latent’s maximum activation scale and breaks down at five times that scale; in the parts we read,
they do not compare a sparse surrogate against the gradient at large scale. Our Taylor comparator
linearizes only the branch vector and keeps the exact nonlinear suffix, so its large-dose failure is
not a scalar first-order artifact. Bazán Sánchez [2] predicts full feature removal from a measured
midpoint inside replacement models; our predictions use no measured intermediate effect and are
scored against native interventions. Strength controls change sparse-code comparisons [12], and
matched-effect evaluation [8] motivates our explicit statement of what fixed-norm controls do and
do not equalize. Deshpande [3] asks which readouts survive sparse compression, a complementary

question about readouts rather than intervention deltas.

3.2 Artifacts, baselines and cohorts
The main artifact is the public medium Gemma-3-270M layer-15 transcoder (no affine skip; the
native model has no final-logit softcap; FP32 runtime with qualified prefix/suffix parity). A vector-
only FIT of 32 name-pair blocks fixesw, class-cell means and a ridge map (centered intercept, global
input RMS,λ=.01, fitted separately per template, operator and dose on 64 FIT rows, so with
far fewer examples than dimensions); a frozen-mask predictor keeps the baseline feature mask. No
baseline uses TEST deltas or readout labels. After a 32-block PILOT, the TEST has 256 disjoint
name-pair blocks, two sides and three templates (1,536 recipients); the so/when mixture is primary
and the third template is predeclared transfer. For the transfer template, the learned baselines are
a single ridge map and class mean fitted on the pooled so/when FIT rows (equal template weights),
frozen before PILOT; no transfer-template labels are used. Each block pairs a male and a female
name in otherwise identical contexts (e.g., “Ivor/Chelsey was tired after the long trip, so”; transfer:
“...put the travel bag beside the door because”); each side is once recipient and once donor, and
the edit acts at the final token.
The second artifact, the public Gemma-3-1B layer-22 medium 16k transcoder (L0 = 60, no affine
skip), was chosen by an availability/depth rule before new outcomes, with 32 fresh FIT and 256
further TEST blocks, the full operator and all six doses, scored through layers 23–25 and the head.
Both artifacts were explored historically before these frozen lexical tests. In particular, Gemma-
270M/L15 and the pronoun task were selected after an exploratory matrix of 36 Gemma transcoder

3.3 Inference
The 270M TEST is a fixed-Nstudy. Its original hypothesis family failed a synthetic error-rate
qualification (52 of 54 scenarios passed; all were required), so we report effect sizes rather than family
rejections; a separate pre-TEST amendment qualified 99% paired studentized log-RMS intervals for
the 12 full-dose TC metrics. Gemma-1B uses nominal 95% block-bootstrap intervals conditional on
its FIT objects. Diagnostics are descriptive.

4.2 Gemma-1B: the crossover repeats, the accuracy does not
The second artifact repeats Taylor’s small-dose advantage and large-dose failure, but at the full
patch its transcoder error, 115.38% (nominal interval [112.72%, 118.25%]), exceeds zero change,
ridge and the class mean (Table 3). In absolute terms it is .4406 logits against a native signal
of .3819. At dose 2 it beats zero change and has a point error close to the class mean (41.86%

5.1 Endpoint cancellation
With endpoint reconstruction errorse0 =T(z)−f(z)ande 1 =T(z+δ)−f(z+δ), the predicted
delta error ise 1 −e 0, with energyE∥e 1∥2 +E∥e 0∥2 −2E(e ⊤

centering within each template, the remaining cross contribution slightly increases the delta error.
A shared offset cancels in any predictor evaluated as a difference, so this alone does not explain why
this artifact is accurate. These statistics pool all three templates. Cancellation shrinks the vector
error, but the vector residual still grows with dose (RMS 95.3 atc=.05to 840.6 atc= 1), so the
readout result also depends on how the native suffix filters that residual: atc= 1the natural-point
gradient term has RMS .267 logits against an observed readout-error RMS of .345, with a nonlinear
remainder of RMS .127.
5.2 Residual direction and threshold switches
On 32 previously observed blocks (768 conditions), replacing each full-dose residual with a partner
residual of the same norm (cyclic shifts 1 and 7) increases readout MSE from .11153 to .14940/.18659
for full edits and from .12119 to .23641/.25200 for projected edits; every full-dose frame favors the
original direction. The transcoder’s residual is thus oriented favorably for this readout, not merely
small.
A mask decomposition shows where the large-dose response is carried. Writing the endpoint
code as a base term with the baseline mask plus the contribution of features that switch, we either
keep only the switches of 32 FIT-selected features (ONLY) or all switches except theirs (EXCEPT).
At full dose, ONLY gives MSE .10463, essentially the original .11153, while EXCEPT gives 8.93294
(projected: .11782 and 7.45841 versus .12119). The selected group contributes on average 8.3
threshold crossings per context at full dose (4.1 for projected edits); ONLY retains the whole base
code, so the result shows that these switches are necessary for accuracy rather than that they alone
produce it. The fixed random 32-feature group rarely switches (no switches in 188/192 contexts)
and is not a strength-matched control.

7 Discussion
Local fidelity, ranking against local linearization and finite-effect accuracy are three different prop-
erties of a replacement model. The two Gemma artifacts tested under the frozen protocol separate
them in different ways, whereas the Qwen component is poor on all three. The Gemma-270M
transcoder is a poor local model yet predicts full-patch effects to about 10%, better in readout than
a ridge map that is closer in vector space; the Gemma-1B transcoder shows the same crossover
without that accuracy; the Qwen CLT component shows neither. For anyone using a transcoder
to predict or choose interventions, the practical consequence is that fidelity should be measured
at the intervention size of interest, in the readout of interest and against learned and zero-change
baselines, rather than inferred from reconstruction, Jacobians or superiority to Taylor.
The scope is one historically selected public artifact with a clear positive result, one replication
of the crossover, synthetic pronoun contexts and two-token readouts; the original hypothesis family
was not qualified, and the mechanism diagnostics are posthoc. Within that scope the first artifact’s
success is associated with a favorably oriented residual and the threshold switches of one FIT-
selected group, while endpoint-error cancellation alone does not explain it; a predictive account
of which artifacts reach this regime remains open. Complete dose grids, earlier hypotheses and
failed forecasts, operation accounting and independent reviews are in the supplement. Complete
